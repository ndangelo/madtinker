<section class="ag-hero">
<p class="ag-eyebrow">AMDE 355 · Advanced Web Design · Week 04</p>
<h2>Visual Research &amp; Art Direction.</h2>
<p>Strong web design starts before the first line of code. This week, you’ll gather visual references, identify the decisions that make them effective, and translate those observations into a clear art direction for a website.</p>
<div class="ag-chips"><span class="ag-chip">Visual research</span><span class="ag-chip">Moodboards</span><span class="ag-chip">Typography &amp; color</span><span class="ag-chip">Art direction</span></div>
</section>
<section class="ag-section">
<h2>01 / The challenge — turn inspiration into direction</h2>
<p>A moodboard can collect interesting images, but art direction gives those references a purpose. You are looking for patterns in composition, typography, color, image treatment, rhythm, and interaction—and then deciding which ideas belong together in your own design.</p>
<div class="ag-grid">
<article class="ag-card"><span class="ag-number">01</span><h3>Research with a question</h3><p>Gather references that help answer a design problem rather than collecting images only because they look appealing.</p></article>
<article class="ag-card"><span class="ag-number">02</span><h3>Identify visual patterns</h3><p>Look closely at hierarchy, alignment, contrast, scale, density, and the way images and type work together.</p></article>
<article class="ag-card"><span class="ag-number">03</span><h3>Make deliberate rules</h3><p>Turn what you learn into a direction that can guide a consistent, usable website—not just one attractive screen.</p></article>
</div>
<div class="ag-callout"><strong>Design question:</strong> What specific visual decisions communicate the mood or message you want? Replace vague words like “cool” or “modern” with observable choices you can explain.</div>
</section>
<section class="ag-section">
<h2>02 / Read a visual reference</h2>
<p>When you find a reference, describe what it does. A useful annotation tells you what to study and how that observation might influence your own work.</p>
<div class="ag-grid">
<article class="ag-card"><h3>Composition &amp; grid</h3><p>Where does the eye land first? Is the layout balanced, asymmetric, modular, crowded, or deliberately irregular?</p></article>
<article class="ag-card"><h3>Typography</h3><p>How do size, weight, width, alignment, and line length establish hierarchy and pace?</p></article>
<article class="ag-card"><h3>Color &amp; contrast</h3><p>Does color organize information, create atmosphere, highlight a key action, or separate sections?</p></article>
<article class="ag-card"><h3>Image treatment</h3><p>Notice crops, repetition, scale, overlays, grain, aspect ratios, and how images interact with surrounding text.</p></article>
<article class="ag-card"><h3>Spacing &amp; rhythm</h3><p>How do margins, gaps, repetition, and changes in density affect the reading experience?</p></article>
<article class="ag-card"><h3>Interaction cues</h3><p>How does the design communicate navigation, links, buttons, hover states, or what can be clicked?</p></article>
</div>
<div class="ag-callout blue"><strong>Example annotation:</strong> Instead of “I like this poster,” write “The oversized condensed headline creates urgency, while the small caption and large margins give the image room to breathe.”</div>
</section>
<section class="ag-section">
<h2>03 / Build a focused moodboard</h2>
<p>Gather a small, varied set of references. Your goal is to discover a direction, not to prove how many images you can collect.</p>
<ol class="ag-steps">
<li><strong>Define the design problem.</strong><br/>Write one sentence describing the project, audience, and intended experience.</li>
<li><strong>Collect 8–12 references.</strong><br/>Mix websites with posters, editorial design, photography, typography, objects, or other relevant visual material.</li>
<li><strong>Annotate each reference.</strong><br/>Identify one or two specific decisions worth studying and record the source link and creator when available.</li>
<li><strong>Group related observations.</strong><br/>Sort the material into themes such as composition, type, color, image treatment, and interaction.</li>
<li><strong>Remove distractions.</strong><br/>Delete references that do not support the direction. A tighter board is often more useful than a larger one.</li>
</ol>
<div class="ag-callout amber"><strong>Research ethics:</strong> credit your sources and use references to understand design decisions—not to copy another designer’s finished website or visual identity.</div>
</section>
<section class="ag-section">
<h2>04 / Translate research into design rules</h2>
<p>A clear direction gives you practical decisions you can reuse as you design and code. Use the example below as a starting point, then adapt it to your own project.</p>
<div class="ag-grid">
<article class="ag-card"><span class="ag-number">A</span><h3>Keywords</h3><p>Choose three to five words that describe the intended experience, such as tactile, restless, precise, quiet, or energetic.</p></article>
<article class="ag-card"><span class="ag-number">B</span><h3>Type system</h3><p>Define a display style, a readable body style, and a clear relationship between headings, captions, and supporting text.</p></article>
<article class="ag-card"><span class="ag-number">C</span><h3>Color system</h3><p>Choose a small palette and decide what each color does. An accent should have a job, not appear everywhere by default.</p></article>
<article class="ag-card"><span class="ag-number">D</span><h3>Layout system</h3><p>Describe your grid, alignment, spacing, image ratios, and where you will allow asymmetry or visual tension.</p></article>
</div>
<h3>Example CSS design tokens</h3>

<p>Tokens help repeat the same decisions across a page. They make a visual direction easier to maintain when the design grows or needs revision.</p>
</section>
<section class="ag-section">
<h2>05 / In-class exercise — three directions, one subject</h2>
<p>Choose a photographer, musician, exhibition, publication, or fictional creative studio. Keep the subject and content the same, then create three distinct visual directions. This makes it easier to compare the design decisions themselves.</p>
<div class="ag-grid">
<article class="ag-card"><span class="ag-number">01</span><h3>Editorial</h3><p>Use considered typography, clear reading order, intentional margins, and a deliberate pace between sections.</p></article>
<article class="ag-card"><span class="ag-number">02</span><h3>Utility / system</h3><p>Use modular structure, labels, consistent alignment, and functional color to prioritize clarity and scanning.</p></article>
<article class="ag-card"><span class="ag-number">03</span><h3>Experimental</h3><p>Explore asymmetry, unusual scale, layered imagery, or controlled tension while keeping the content understandable.</p></article>
</div>
<h3>What to produce</h3>
<ul class="ag-checklist">
<li>A one-sentence project brief and audience description.</li>
<li>8–12 visual references with short annotations and source links.</li>
<li>Three mini moodboards or direction frames.</li>
<li>A short rationale comparing the three approaches.</li>
<li>One selected direction with initial type, color, image, and layout rules.</li>
</ul>
<div class="ag-callout"><strong>Success criteria:</strong> the directions should feel meaningfully different, your references should support the choices, and your selected direction should be specific enough to guide an actual website.</div>
</section>
<section class="ag-section">
<h2>06 / Critique the direction</h2>
<ul class="ag-checklist">
<li>What do you notice first, and which decision makes it dominant?</li>
<li>Which references best support the direction? Are any included only because they look attractive?</li>
<li>What connects the typography, color, image treatment, and composition?</li>
<li>Could another designer use these rules to create a second page that feels related?</li>
<li>What would need to change on a narrow screen while keeping the same visual character?</li>
</ul>
</section>
<section class="ag-section">
<h2>07 / What to submit</h2>
<ul class="ag-checklist">
<li>A visual research board with 8–12 credited references.</li>
<li>Three distinct art-direction frames using the same subject.</li>
<li>Annotations that describe specific design decisions.</li>
<li>A short comparison and rationale for the direction you selected.</li>
<li>A concise set of reusable visual rules for type, color, spacing, images, and layout.</li>
</ul>
</section>

<h3>Step 6 — Test and discuss</h3>
<ol class="ag-steps">
<li>Resize the browser and check that the cards wrap without horizontal scrolling.</li>
<li>Change the accent color and discuss how it affects the page’s tone and hierarchy.</li>
<li>Change the title scale and check whether the reading order remains clear.</li>
<li>Ask students to make a second direction by changing only two or three rules.</li>
<li>Discuss which rules should remain consistent if the page gains more sections.</li>
</ol>
<div class="ag-callout blue"><strong>Instructor note:</strong> The goal is not to teach one “correct” aesthetic. Model how to connect a visual decision to an intention, then invite students to test alternative choices and explain their effects.</div>
