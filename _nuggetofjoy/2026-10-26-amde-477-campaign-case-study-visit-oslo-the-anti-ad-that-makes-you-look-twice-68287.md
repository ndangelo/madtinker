<p class="kicker">AMDE 477 / Campaign Case Study</p>
<h1>VisitOSLO: The Anti-Ad That Makes You Look Twice</h1>
<p>Study how irony, visual contrast, and an unexpected point of view can make a tourism campaign memorable.</p>
</header>
<p>In 2024, VisitOSLO released “Is it even a city?”, a tourism video built around a local resident named Halfdan, who appears to criticize Oslo in a dry, deadpan voice. As he complains about the city being too accessible, too easy to navigate, or insufficiently exclusive, the viewer sees features that may actually make Oslo appealing.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8vhD59ac7nw" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Watch the official VisitOSLO video on YouTube</a> before reading the analysis. Notice what the video shows while the narrator says the opposite.</p>
<div class="callout"><strong>Campaign idea:</strong> Reverse the expected tone of the category. Instead of using a glossy travel pitch, the campaign uses self-deprecating humor to make Oslo’s qualities stand out.</div>
<h2>1 / The objective and audience</h2>
<p>VisitOSLO’s campaign was designed to build awareness of Oslo as a leisure and business-event destination. Its official marketing organization used humor and irony to help the city stand out in a competitive tourism landscape.</p>
<p>The video can appeal to viewers who appreciate understated humor, authenticity, and travel experiences that feel less crowded or less intimidating than major tourist capitals. That audience interpretation is a hypothesis to discuss—not proof that every viewer shares those preferences.</p>
<h2>2 / How the creative works</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Start with a surprising statement.</strong> The opening sounds like a warning not to visit.</li>
<li><strong>Use contrast.</strong> The narration is negative, while the visuals reveal culture, waterfront spaces, art, restaurants, and urban life.</li>
<li><strong>Build a character.</strong> Halfdan’s deadpan delivery provides a consistent point of view.</li>
<li><strong>Let the audience connect the dots.</strong> The viewer recognizes that the complaints may actually be selling points.</li>
<li><strong>Invite conversation.</strong> The unusual approach gives people something to discuss and share.</li>
</ol>
<div class="example"><div class="label">What the viewer hears versus sees</div>
<p><strong>Voice:</strong> Oslo is too easy; everything is available.</p>
<p><strong>Visual implication:</strong> Visitors can access cultural attractions, restaurants, public spaces, and the city without the barriers associated with some larger destinations.</p>
<p><strong>Effect:</strong> The audience interprets the mismatch between words and images as the joke—and discovers the benefits in the process.</p>
</div>
<h2>3 / Why the approach stands out</h2>
<div class="grid">
<div class="card"><h3>It breaks category expectations</h3><p>Many tourism ads use aspirational images and enthusiastic narration. This campaign takes a deliberately different route.</p></div>
<div class="card"><h3>The idea is easy to explain</h3><p>“An anti-ad that makes you want to visit” is a compact concept people can repeat and discuss.</p></div>
<div class="card"><h3>The tone supports the message</h3><p>The dry delivery and understated visuals work together; the humor is not simply a joke added to a conventional tourism ad.</p></div>
<div class="card"><h3>The creative carries the argument</h3><p>Rather than listing every benefit, the video lets the viewer infer the appeal from the contrast.</p></div>
</div>
<h2>4 / Could your brand use this approach?</h2>
<p>Reverse psychology is not a formula to copy. It depends on the audience understanding the irony and trusting that the brand is in control of the joke. If viewers take the negative message literally, the campaign may backfire.</p>
<div class="example"><div class="label">Fictional classroom example</div>
<p>A small independent bookstore might make a short video titled “Definitely don’t come here if you dislike getting distracted,” showing a reader discovering unexpected books, staff recommendations, and a comfortable reading corner.</p>
<p>The joke works only if the visuals make the intended positive meaning clear. The CTA could be “Visit this week and find your next read.”</p>
</div>
<h2>5 / Adapt the idea for four platforms</h2>
<div class="grid">
<div class="card"><h3>Facebook</h3><p>Share the video with a short introduction that invites local audiences to comment on the unexpected benefits of the place or service.</p></div>
<div class="card"><h3>Instagram</h3><p>Use a Reel built around the contrast between deadpan narration and appealing visuals. A carousel could pair “complaint” lines with the real benefit.</p></div>
<div class="card"><h3>X</h3><p>Use one concise, ironic line paired with a visual that makes the intended meaning clear. Avoid ambiguity if the post is viewed out of context.</p></div>
<div class="card"><h3>TikTok</h3><p>Open with the surprising complaint, then reveal the positive reality through quick visual examples and a final CTA.</p></div>
</div>
<h2>6 / What evidence would you examine?</h2>
<p>The number of views can show exposure, but it does not automatically prove that people became more likely to visit. For a tourism campaign, additional measures might include shares, comments, searches for the destination, visits to travel-planning pages, referral traffic, or later tourism research. Be careful to distinguish correlation from proof of causation.</p>
<h2>Discuss and apply</h2>
<ol>
<li>What does the video want you to believe about Oslo?</li>
<li>How do the words and visuals work together?</li>
<li>What makes the irony understandable rather than confusing?</li>
<li>Would this tone fit your brand? What evidence would help you decide?</li>
</ol>
<h3>Mini activity</h3>
<p>Write a deliberately unexpected opening line for your campaign. Then storyboard three visuals that reveal the real benefit. Ask a classmate whether they understand the intended meaning without additional explanation.</p>
